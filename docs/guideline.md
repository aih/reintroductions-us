# Reintroducing a bill in a new Congress

A bill that does not become law dies when the Congress that received it adjourns.
To pursue it in the next Congress, a Member introduces it again as a new bill with
a new number. This guide lists what changes between the two texts and what to check
before the new one is introduced.

The figures come from 1,046 bills introduced in January 2025 (119th Congress) whose
introduced text was compared with the introduced text of the same bill in the 118th
Congress. The method and the data are in the [research report](/research).

## Check first

- **Whether the bill became law.** 269 of the 16,213 House and Senate bills of the
  118th Congress were enacted. This site marks them and does not prepare a
  reintroduction for them.
- **Whether its text became law inside another bill.** Provisions of a stand-alone
  bill are often enacted as a section of a larger law (a defense authorization, an
  appropriations act, a tax bill). The bill's own status then reads "referred to
  committee". Search the laws enacted since the bill's last action for its short
  title and for the provisions it amends.
- **Whether it has already been reintroduced.** Search the current Congress for the
  bill's short title (with any year removed) and its official title.

## The starting text

Use the most advanced version of the bill that did not become law. 175 of the
1,046 bills had a version later than the introduced one in the 118th Congress (a
reported or engrossed text); 85 of those 175 were reintroduced closer to the later
version than to the introduced one.

A reported version marks committee amendments as added and deleted text. The
starting text is the bill as amended: added text kept, deleted text dropped. An
engrossed version is already in that form.

## The heading of the bill

These parts are assigned at introduction and are not carried over:

| Part | In the new bill |
|---|---|
| Congress and session | The new Congress; 1st session for a bill introduced in its first year |
| Bill number | Assigned by the clerk at introduction |
| Date of introduction | The day it is introduced |
| Sponsor | The Member introducing it. 85.9% of the bills had the same sponsor as before; 43 moved from one chamber to the other |
| Cosponsors | Collected again. Original cosponsors of the new bill included 44.6% of the old bill's cosponsors |
| Committee referral | Made by the Parliamentarian at introduction. 94.4% went to the same committees |

The official title ("To amend the ... and for other purposes") was unchanged in
89.8% of the bills.

## The short title

A short title that ends in a year carries the year of introduction:
"WEATHER Act of 2023" became "WEATHER Act of 2025" (S. 231, 119th Congress). Of
the 1,046 bills, 57.7% kept every short title unchanged and 31.4% changed only the
year.

The bill often repeats its own short title, for example in "the amendments made by
the Landlord Accountability Act of 2023". Every occurrence changes with the title.

Some sponsors drop the year instead: "Congressional Award Program Reauthorization
Act of 2024" became "Congressional Award Program Reauthorization Act" (H.R. 860).

## Years and dates in the text

451 of the 1,046 bills changed at least one year in their operative text. Most
moved forward by two years (652 changed years) or one (394).

| Kind of date | Example (118th → 119th) | Bills changed / bills with one |
|---|---|---|
| Effective date for taxable years | "taxable years beginning after December 31, **2023**" → **2025** (H.R. 206) | 48 / 55 |
| Authorization of appropriations | "for each of fiscal years **2024** through **2033**" → **2026** through **2035** (H.R. 206) | 80 / 120 |
| Fixed deadlines | "Not later than October 1, **2026**" → **2027** (H.R. 683) | 15 / 22 |
| Retroactive effective date | "If this Act is enacted after October 1, 2024, ..." removed (H.R. 860) | |

Move a date when it is a date the bill sets: when a program starts, how long it is
funded, when a report is due, which tax years a change applies to. Keep the length
of an authorization period unless the sponsor wants it changed: a five-year period
that began in fiscal year 2025 begins in the first fiscal year after the new bill
could be enacted.

Do not move these:

- Years in the names of other laws: "National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal
  Year 2021", "Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021".
- Dates of past events in findings, unless the finding is rewritten.
- Dates in the current law that the bill strikes; those must match the law as it
  reads now (see the next section).
- Citations to Public Laws and to the Statutes at Large.

## Amendments to existing law

632 of the 1,046 bills amend existing law, and 395 do it by striking and inserting
words. Between Congresses, the law the bill amends may itself have been amended.
For each amended provision:

- **Is the provision still there?** A section can be repealed, transferred or
  redesignated. An amendment to a provision that no longer exists cannot be
  executed.
- **Is the struck text still in the law?** H.R. 1514 (118th) struck "$1,388.68" from
  the Medal of Honor pension in 38 U.S.C. 1562; the cost-of-living adjustment had
  changed the amount, and H.R. 695 (119th) strikes "$1,406.73". H.R. 695 became
  Public Law 119-43, which replaced the amount with a rate, so neither figure is in
  the section now. H.R. 860 strikes
  "October 1, 2023" where its 118th version struck "October 1, 2024", because the
  date in current law had changed.
- **Has the provision changed around the amendment?** A new paragraph inserted by
  another law changes the paragraph numbers an instruction such as "by
  redesignating paragraph (4) as paragraph (5)" relies on.
- **Has the amendment already been made?** A provision of the bill that another law
  has already enacted is dropped.

This site looks up each section of the United States Code the bill cites, on
[uscode.linkedlegislation.org](https://uscode.linkedlegislation.org), and reports
the laws classified to it since the old bill's text was written, whether its text
has changed, and whether the words the bill strikes are still in it.

## Citations

769 of the 1,046 bills cite the United States Code or a Public Law; 91 changed at
least one citation. Check each citation for:

- A section that was repealed, transferred, renumbered or omitted.
- A section the classification tables show amended since the old bill was written.
- A Public Law cited by number whose relevant section was amended later.
- "et seq." citations to a chapter that was reorganized.

## Names of committees, agencies and officers

A bill that directs a report to a committee names the committee as it is called in
the new Congress. At the start of the 119th Congress the House Committee on
Oversight and Accountability became the Committee on Oversight and Government
Reform; 39 bills introduced in the 119th Congress still name the old committee,
7 of them in January 2025. Check agency and officer titles the same way.

## Findings, statistics and amounts

Findings that cite statistics, reports or recent events are updated or dropped.
Appropriated amounts that refer to a baseline year are recomputed: H.R. 869 (Keep
Our PACT Act) moved its base year from fiscal year 2023 to 2025 and changed the
amounts.

## Structure

77 of the 1,046 bills added or removed a section. After any change:

- The table of contents matches the sections.
- Cross references inside the bill ("section 4(b)") point to the right provision.
- Section numbering is consecutive.

## Checklist

1. The bill did not become law, alone or inside another law.
2. The starting text is the most advanced version, with committee amendments applied.
3. Congress, session, date, sponsor, cosponsors and referral are left for the new
   introduction.
4. Short titles carry the new year, everywhere they appear.
5. Effective dates, authorization periods and deadlines are moved; names of other
   laws and dates of past events are not.
6. Every amended provision exists, the struck text is in it, and the amendment has
   not already been made.
7. Every citation resolves to a provision in force.
8. Committee, agency and officer names are current.
9. Findings and amounts are current.
10. The table of contents and internal cross references match the text.

## What this site does

For a bill of the most recent Congress that did not become law, the site produces
a draft in USLM 2.0 XML that:

- carries the new Congress and session, with the bill number, date, sponsor,
  cosponsors and referral left empty;
- moves the year in the bill's own short titles, everywhere they occur;
- marks, in XML comments shown on the page, each place that needs a person: a
  cited section amended since the old text, struck words not found in current law,
  a date the bill sets, a fiscal year already begun, a renamed committee.

It does not change operative dates or amounts itself. The PDF of the draft is
marked "Draft — not for introduction" on every page.
